PART 1 — PROJECT OVERVIEW
================================================================================

1. PROJECT TITLE
   Smart Health Dashboard — AI-Powered Personalised Nutrition and Disease
   Risk Analysis System
   (Actual name shown on app: "Smart Health Dashboard")

2. MAIN OBJECTIVE
   Predict the risk of Diabetes, Obesity, and Kidney Disease from patient
   health parameters, and then automatically generate a personalised daily
   meal plan that is appropriate for the predicted diseases and their severity.

3. PROBLEM BEING SOLVED
   Patients with chronic diseases (diabetes, obesity, kidney disease) are often
   unsure what to eat. Generic diet charts do not account for the specific
   disease, its severity, or the individual's body metrics. There is a need for
   a system that:
     a. Automatically detects the disease from health parameters.
     b. Estimates how severe it is.
     c. Recommends safe, appropriate foods tailored to that severity.

4. EXISTING SYSTEM (LIMITATIONS)
   - Manual diet charts given by doctors are generic, not personalised.
   - DSS-based systems (like the base paper) use only rule-based logic,
     no machine learning for disease prediction.
   - DSS systems require the disease to be manually entered; they do not
     predict it automatically.
   - Rule-only systems cannot score foods numerically or rank them.

5. PROPOSED SYSTEM
   A web-based dashboard that:
     a. Collects patient inputs (age, gender, BMI, HbA1c, blood glucose,
        sodium, potassium, BP, creatinine).
     b. Uses three separate trained Random Forest classifiers to predict
        Diabetes, Obesity, and Kidney Disease automatically.
     c. Derives severity (Mild / Moderate / Severe) from the model's
        risk classification combined with clinical marker analysis.
     d. Uses a Hybrid Scoring Recommendation Engine to score every food
        in the dataset based on the predicted disease, severity, BMI,
        TDEE, and nutritional content.
     e. Generates a daily meal plan (Breakfast / Lunch / Snack / Dinner)
        with the highest-scoring, disease-appropriate foods.

6. COMPLETE PROJECT WORKFLOW

   USER INPUT (Age, Gender, Height, Weight, HbA1c, Glucose, BP, Sodium,
               Potassium, Creatinine, Activity Level)
       ↓
   UTILITY CALCULATIONS (BMI, BMR, TDEE — utils.py)
       ↓
   ML DISEASE PREDICTION (predict.py + models.py)
       → Obesity Model: RandomForestClassifier (3 features, 7 classes)
       → Diabetes Model: RandomForestClassifier (5 features, 8 classes)
       → Kidney Model:   RandomForestClassifier (7 features, 3 classes)
       ↓
   RISK CLASSIFICATION (backend/risk.py — classify_risk())
       → Combines ML probability with clinical reference ranges
       → Outputs: High Risk / Moderate Risk / Borderline / Model Flag / Low Risk
       ↓
   SEVERITY MAPPING (backend/services/severity_rules.py — build_severity_dict())
       → High Risk     → severe
       → Moderate Risk → moderate
       → Borderline / Model Flag → mild
       → Low Risk      → None (no disease restriction)
       ↓
   DISEASE HARD FILTERING (enhanced_recommender.py — _apply_disease_filters())
       → Remove foods violating base disease thresholds (config.py)
       ↓
   SEVERITY HARD FILTERING (enhanced_recommender.py — _apply_severity_filters())
       → Apply tighter per-severity nutritional bounds from severity_rules.py
       ↓
   DYNAMIC NORMALIZATION
       → Compute p99 of nutrition scores in this candidate pool as ceiling
       ↓
   HYBRID SCORING (enhanced_recommender.py — _calculate_hybrid_score())
       → Nutrition Score (severity-multiplied, demographic-adjusted)
       → Severity Suitability Score (soft constraint penalty, 0–1)
       → Content Score (calorie-fit to TDEE, protein density, fiber density, sugar)
       → Hybrid = 0.625 × Nutrition_blended + 0.375 × Content_score
       ↓
   TOP-N SELECTION PER MEAL SLOT (deterministic sort by hybrid score)
       → Breakfast: top 2 | Lunch: top 3 | Snack: top 2 | Dinner: top 3
       ↓
   MEAL PLAN OUTPUT + foods_to_avoid + water_intake + protein_requirement
       ↓
   STREAMLIT UI (app.py) — Meal plan displayed with food cards

Algorithm name:      Rule-Based Risk Classification (DSS component)
Where implemented:   backend/risk.py — classify_risk() function
File:                backend/risk.py
Function/Class:      classify_risk()
Purpose:             Combine ML prediction probability with clinical reference

Input:               disease_key, model_label, model_probability,
                     clinical_values dict (bmi, hba1c, glucose, sodium, etc.)
Output:              RiskResult dataclass with final_status, card_level,

Purpose:             Convert RiskResult.final_status → Mild/Moderate/Severe

    "Low Risk"             → None (no restriction)

---[ ALGORITHM 6 — HYBRID SCORING RECOMMENDATION ]---
Algorithm name:      Hybrid Content-Based Food Recommendation with Severity Weighting
Where implemented:   backend/services/enhanced_recommender.py
File:                enhanced_recommender.py
Function/Class:      EnhancedNutritionRecommender class, generate_meal_plan(),
                     _calculate_hybrid_score()
Purpose:             Score all candidate foods and select top-N per meal slot
Input:               user_profile dict (diseases, severity, BMI, age, gender,
                     activity_level, daily_calories, etc.)
                     food_dataset.csv rows (candidate foods)
Output:              Daily meal plan dict with top-scoring foods per meal type
How it works:
    Step 1: Filter food dataset by disease hard constraints (config.py thresholds)
    Step 2: Apply severity-level hard constraints (severity_rules.py)
    Step 3: Compute dynamic p99 normalisation ceiling
    Step 4: For each candidate food compute:
              a. Nutrition Score = severity-multiplied arithmetic score
                 (penalties for sugar/carbs/sodium/fat; bonuses for fiber/protein)
                 + demographic adjustments (age, gender, BMI, activity)
              b. Severity Suitability Score = soft-constraint penalty in [0,1]
                 from calculate_severity_suitability_score()
              c. nutrition_blended = 0.70 × norm(nutrition_raw) + 0.30 × suitability
              d. Content Score = calorie-fit (40%) + protein density (25%)
                                 + fibre density (20%) + sugar inverse (15%)
              e. hybrid_score = 0.625 × nutrition_blended + 0.375 × content_score
    Step 5: Per meal slot — apply diversity penalty for already-selected foods,
            sort descending by adjusted_score, select top-N
NOT collaborative filtering (no user history used at runtime).
NOT deep learning — fully deterministic arithmetic scoring.
This is a Weighted Hybrid Content-Based Recommendation System.

PART 3 — BASE PAPER vs MY PROJECT COMPARISON
================================================================================

BASE PAPER APPROACH:
The base paper uses a DSS (Decision Support System). In a typical DSS for
healthcare nutrition:
- The disease is ENTERED MANUALLY by the doctor or patient.
- The system applies fixed rule tables (if disease=X then avoid food Y).
- No machine learning is involved in disease prediction.
- Recommendations are static lookup tables, not scored or ranked.
- Severity is either not handled or manually set.
- Output is a fixed diet plan from a knowledge base.

MY PROJECT APPROACH:
- Diseases are predicted AUTOMATICALLY using trained Random Forest classifiers.
- Severity is derived automatically from the ML model's risk output.
- Food scoring is numerical and personalised (hybrid score considers BMI,
  TDEE, severity multipliers, calorie fit, protein density).
- Recommendations change dynamically with each user's profile.
- A SQLite database stores history for potential future NCF training.

The evaluation script (evaluate_models.py) searched for a real test dataset
but did NOT find one in the repository (no train/test CSV files present).
Therefore, it generated SYNTHETIC test data using random.uniform() for
each feature within realistic ranges, and evaluated the models on that
synthetic data with TEST_SIZE = 0.2.

This means: the metrics below reflect model behaviour on RANDOMLY GENERATED
inputs, NOT on a real held-out patient dataset.

---
Q: "Why did you use this algorithm?"
EASY:   "Random Forest was chosen because it handles both continuous and
         categorical inputs, does not require feature scaling, handles
         multi-class classification naturally, gives a probability estimate
         (predict_proba) for confidence display, and is resistant to
         overfitting via ensemble averaging."
TECH:   "Each medical prediction task involves non-linear boundaries between
         disease classes. Random Forest creates multiple decision trees on
         bootstrap samples and averages their predictions, reducing variance.
         It provides feature importance for explainability, tolerates feature
         collinearity (age/BMI correlation), and does not require normalisation
         — important for a system handling diverse medical measurements."

---
Q: "Where exactly did you use ML?"
EASY:   "ML is used ONLY for disease prediction. Three Random Forest models
         predict Obesity, Diabetes, and Kidney Disease from the patient's

TECH:   "predict.py calls models.obesity_model.predict(), models.disease_model
         .predict(), models.kidney_model.predict(). The ML output (label +
         confidence probability) feeds into backend/risk.py's classify_risk()
         which is rule-based. The recommendation system in enhanced_recommender
         .py is entirely arithmetic/rule-based."

---
Q: "Your base paper uses DSS. Why did you use ML?"
EASY:   "The base paper's DSS requires the user or doctor to manually enter
         the disease diagnosis. We replaced that manual step with automatic
         disease prediction using ML, which makes the system more practical —

TECH:   "In the base DSS, disease type is a manual input to the rule engine.
         We inserted an ML classification layer (Random Forest) before the
         DSS reasoning step. The ML models take raw clinical measurements
         (HbA1c, glucose, creatinine, etc.) and output a disease label. That
         label then enters the same kind of rule-based reasoning that a DSS

PART 7 — DISEASE SEVERITY
================================================================================

HOW SEVERITY IS CALCULATED (actual code flow):

Step 1 — ML prediction gives a risk label
   predict_diabetes() → label e.g. "Diabetes" with probability e.g. 78.5%

Step 2 — backend/risk.py classify_risk()
   Rule-based: checks model label + clinical values vs reference ranges
   Outputs one of:
     "High Risk"     — model predicts disease + clinical markers abnormal
     "Moderate Risk" — model predicts disease + borderline markers
     "Model Flag"    — model predicts disease but all markers normal
     "Low Risk"      — model predicts no disease + all markers normal
     "Borderline"    — model predicts no disease but some markers borderline

Step 3 — severity_rules.py build_severity_dict() / final_status_to_severity()
   Pure rule mapping:
     "High Risk"             → "severe"
     "Moderate Risk"         → "moderate"
     "Borderline"/"Model Flag" → "mild"
     "Low Risk"              → None (no restrictions)
     Missing/unknown         → "moderate" (safe default from config.SEVERITY_DEFAULT)

WHICH FEATURES DETERMINE SEVERITY:
The clinical reference ranges checked (CLINICAL_RANGES in risk.py):
   BMI:      normal=18.5–24.9, borderline=17.0–29.9
   HbA1c:    normal=4.0–5.6%,  borderline=4.0–6.4%
   Glucose:  normal=70–99 mg/dL, borderline=60–125
   Systolic BP: normal=90–119 mmHg, borderline=80–139
   Sodium:   normal=135–145 mEq/L, borderline=130–150
   Potassium: normal=3.5–5.0 mEq/L, borderline=3.0–5.5
   Creatinine: normal=0.5–1.3 mg/dL, borderline=0.4–1.8

IS SEVERITY ML-BASED OR RULE-BASED?
RULE-BASED ONLY. No ML is involved in severity calculation.
Severity is derived from if-else logic in risk.py and severity_rules.py.

HOW SEVERITY AFFECTS RECOMMENDATION:
severity_rules.py defines per-disease, per-tier rules used in recommendation:

HARD CONSTRAINTS (foods violating these are removed entirely):
  Diabetes mild:     sugar≤8g, calories≤350kcal
  Diabetes moderate: sugar≤5g, calories≤250kcal
  Diabetes severe:   sugar≤2g, calories≤200kcal, carbs≤20g

  Obesity mild:      calories≤350kcal, fat≤18g
  Obesity moderate:  calories≤250kcal, fat≤10g
  Obesity severe:    calories≤180kcal, fat≤6g, sugar≤3g

  Kidney mild:       sodium≤150mg, protein≤15g
  Kidney moderate:   sodium≤100mg, protein≤12g
  Kidney severe:     sodium≤60mg,  protein≤8g

SCORING MULTIPLIERS (increase penalty/bonus for higher severity):
  Diabetes mild:    sugar_penalty=3.0, carb_penalty=0.2, fiber_bonus=2.5
  Diabetes moderate: sugar_penalty=4.0, carb_penalty=0.3, fiber_bonus=3.0
  Diabetes severe:  sugar_penalty=6.0, carb_penalty=0.5, fiber_bonus=4.0

SIMPLE EXAMPLE:
  Patient: HbA1c=9.5% (very high), Glucose=280 mg/dL (very high)
  → ML model predicts Diabetes (e.g. 84% confidence)
  → HbA1c=9.5 is above borderline (6.4%) → abnormal marker
  → classify_risk() → "High Risk"
  → final_status_to_severity() → "severe"
  → Hard filter: only foods with sugar≤2g, calories≤200kcal, carbs≤20g
  → Scoring: sugar penalty = 6.0 per gram (vs 3.0 for mild)
  → Result: only very low-sugar, low-calorie foods recommended

PART 8 — RECOMMENDATION SYSTEM
================================================================================

RECOMMENDATION ALGORITHM:
Weighted Hybrid Content-Based Scoring (deterministic, rule-based arithmetic)
Main implementation: backend/services/enhanced_recommender.py
Entry function: generate_meal_plan(user_profile)
Called from: meal_planner.py → generate_comprehensive_recommendations()
             → app.py → generate_comprehensive_recommendations()

STEP-BY-STEP RECOMMENDATION LOGIC:

1. DISEASE HARD FILTERING (_apply_disease_filters)
   Remove foods violating base disease thresholds from config.py:
   Diabetes: sugar>5g OR calories>250kcal → removed
   Kidney:   sodium>100mg OR protein>12g → removed
   Obesity:  calories>250kcal OR fats>10g → removed
   Healthy:  calories>500kcal OR sugar>15g → removed

2. SEVERITY HARD FILTERING (_apply_severity_filters)
   Apply tighter per-tier bounds from severity_rules.py
   (see PART 7 hard constraints table above)
   Pool reduces: e.g., Diabetes mild=425 foods, severe=233 foods

3. DYNAMIC NORMALIZATION
   Compute numpy.percentile(all_nutrition_scores, 99) as ceiling
   Purpose: prevent spice blends with 35g fiber from clamping to 1.0

4. HYBRID SCORE CALCULATION (_calculate_hybrid_score)
   For each candidate food:

   a. Nutrition Score (_calculate_nutrition_score)
      - Reads from food: fiber, protein, sugar, calories, sodium,
        carbs, fats, vit_c, calcium, iron
      - Demographic adjustments:
          Age>65: +protein×1.2, +calcium×0.01
          Age<30: +min(calories/100,5)×0.5
          Male: +protein×1.1
          Female: +iron×2.0
          BMI>25: -calories×0.03, +fiber×2.0
          BMI<18.5: +calories×0.02, +protein×1.5
          Active(mult>1.2): +calories×0.015, +carbs×0.1
      - Disease penalties (severity-multiplied):
          Diabetes: -sugar×penalty, -carbs×penalty, +fiber×bonus
          Kidney:   -sodium×penalty, -protein×penalty
          Obesity:  -calories×penalty, -fats×penalty, +protein×bonus, +fiber×bonus
      - General bonuses (always): +fiber×1.5, +protein×1.2, -sugar×1.5
      - General sodium penalty (non-kidney): sodium>200mg → -(sodium-200)×0.02
      - Micronutrient bonuses: +vit_c×0.1, +calcium×0.01, +iron×0.5

   b. Severity Suitability Score (calculate_severity_suitability_score)
      - Checks soft constraints per tier
      - Score = 1.0 - soft_penalty + required_minimum_bonus, in [0,1]
      - Hard violation → 0.0

   c. Nutrition Blended = 0.70 × norm(nutrition_raw) + 0.30 × suitability

   d. Content Score (_calculate_content_score)
      - meal_target = daily_calories × meal_fraction
        (Breakfast:0.25, Lunch:0.35, Snack:0.15, Dinner:0.25)
      - calorie_fit = max(0, 1 - |food_cal/meal_target - 1|)
      - protein_density = clamp(protein/calories / 0.12, 0, 1)
      - fibre_density = clamp(fiber/calories / 0.06, 0, 1)
      - sugar_score = max(0, 1 - sugar/15)
      - content_score = 0.40×calorie_fit + 0.25×protein_density
                       + 0.20×fibre_density + 0.15×sugar_score

   e. NCF Score = 0.0 (always — model not trained)

   f. Effective weights: w_nutrition=0.625, w_content=0.375, w_ncf=0.0
      hybrid_score = 0.625 × nutrition_blended + 0.375 × content_score

5. DIVERSITY PENALTY (_apply_diversity_penalty)
   If food already selected in a previous meal slot → subtract 0.3
   adjusted_score = hybrid_score - diversity_penalty

6. TOP-N SELECTION (_select_top_foods)
   Sort by adjusted_score descending, take head(n)
   Breakfast: n=2, Snack: n=2, Lunch: n=3, Dinner: n=3
   Total: 10 foods per day

REGIONAL FOOD / SOUTH INDIAN HANDLING:
   The user selects an Indian state/UT.
   The region is stored in user_profile['region'] and passed to the recommender.
   However, the food_dataset.csv does NOT have a Region column.
   Therefore, region is stored but does NOT filter or score foods differently
   in the current implementation. The dataset itself contains South Indian foods
   (dosa, idli, upma, paratha, sambar, etc.) which naturally appear in results.

PERSONALISATION:
   Yes — the system responds to:
   - Specific disease (different candidate pool)
   - Severity tier (different hard/soft constraints)
   - BMI (affects calorie penalty/bonus in nutrition score)
   - Daily calories/TDEE (affects content score calorie_fit)
   - Age (affects protein/calcium/calorie bonuses)
   - Gender (affects iron/protein bonuses)
   - Activity level (affects calorie/carb bonuses)

Q17: What is Random Forest?
Easy: Random Forest builds many decision trees on different random subsets
      of the training data and takes a majority vote for the final prediction.
      It is an ensemble method — combining many weak learners.
Technical: Bagging (Bootstrap Aggregating) + random feature selection at
      each split. Each tree is built on a bootstrap sample of the data.
      Final prediction: mode of all tree predictions (classification).
      Reduces overfitting compared to a single decision tree.

Q18: What is the difference between a Decision Tree and Random Forest?
Easy: Decision Tree is a single tree that can overfit. Random Forest uses
      many trees and averages them, which reduces overfitting and is more
      accurate and stable.

Q19: Why not use Deep Learning for disease prediction?
Easy: The input to our models is only 3–7 structured tabular features.
      Deep learning offers no advantage over Random Forest for such small
      feature sets. Random Forest is faster to train, easier to interpret,
      and gives comparable accuracy on structured tabular data.

   deep learning in practice.

   evaluate_models.py generates N_SAMPLES=5000 synthetic inputs per disease
   using np.random.uniform() in realistic feature ranges, splits 80/20 with
   random_state=42, calls sklearn's accuracy_score(y_test, model.predict
   (X_test)), and saves results to experiments/disease_prediction/*.csv.

7. "Why is accuracy appropriate/not appropriate?"
   Accuracy is a reasonable starting metric for multi-class classification.
   However, macro F1 and ROC-AUC are better for imbalanced classes. In our
   case, the 8-class diabetes model has near-random accuracy on synthetic data —
   this is not an appropriate measure of real clinical accuracy. The actual
   training dataset (used when the models were originally built) would give
   meaningful accuracy numbers.

8. "What is your target variable?"
   For ML models: the disease class label (encoded as integer by LabelEncoder).
   For recommendation: there is no target variable — it is unsupervised
   content-based scoring, not supervised classification.

9. "What are your input features?"
   Obesity: age, gender (encoded), BMI — 3 features
   Diabetes: age, gender (encoded), BMI, HbA1c, blood glucose — 5 features
   Kidney: age, gender (encoded), BMI, sodium, potassium, BP, creatinine — 7 features

10. "How many records did you use?"
    Food dataset: 1,014 records. ML training data: not available in repo.
    Evaluation: 5,000 synthetic samples per disease (generated by evaluate_models.py).

11. "How did you preprocess the data?"
    For ML prediction: gender string → integer via LabelEncoder (gender_encoder.pkl).
    All feature values converted to float. A single-row pd.DataFrame created per
    prediction call. No normalisation/scaling (Random Forest doesn't need it).
    For food dataset: no preprocessing needed at runtime.

12. "How did you handle missing values?"
    Food dataset has no missing values (confirmed). All feature values in the
    recommendation engine pass through _safe_float(value, default=0.0) which
    returns 0.0 for NaN, Inf, or non-numeric values.

13. "How did you handle duplicates?"
    Food dataset has no duplicates (confirmed — 1,014 unique food_ids,
    1,014 unique Dish Names).

14. "How is severity calculated?"
    Rule-based, two-step: (1) backend/risk.py classify_risk() checks ML
    label + clinical markers against reference ranges → outputs final_status
    string. (2) severity_rules.py final_status_to_severity() maps that string
    to mild/moderate/severe/None using a fixed lookup dict.

15. "Is severity prediction ML-based or rule-based?"
    Rule-based only. No ML is involved in severity calculation.

16. "How are recommendations generated?"
    Disease + severity → hard filter food dataset → compute dynamic p99
    ceiling → score each food with hybrid formula (nutrition + content) →
    sort descending → select top-N per meal slot deterministically.

17. "How do you validate recommendations?"
    Automated tests: verify zero hard-constraint violations, correct score
    ordering, determinism (same input → same output), different diseases
    produce different foods, severity tightens the candidate pool.

18. "What is the novelty?"
    (1) Automatic ML disease prediction replaces manual DSS entry.
    (2) Three-tier severity system automatically derived from ML output.
    (3) Hybrid scoring responds to individual BMI, TDEE, age, gender, activity.
    (4) Deterministic, reproducible recommendations (no random shuffling).
    (5) NCF architecture with interaction logging infrastructure for future
        personalisation as users interact with the system.

19. "What happens if the model predicts incorrectly?"
    The clinical marker check in risk.py acts as a safety net. If the model
    predicts disease but all clinical values are normal, final_status =
    "Model Flag" → severity = mild → only mild restrictions applied. This
    prevents aggressive food restrictions for a potential false positive.

20. "Why not use Random Forest?" (they may ask why you DIDN'T choose something else)
    We DID use Random Forest. It was chosen over alternatives because:
    vs Decision Tree: RF reduces overfitting via ensemble averaging.
    vs SVM: RF is faster at prediction, handles multi-class natively.
    vs Logistic Regression: RF handles non-linear decision boundaries.
    vs Neural Network: RF outperforms NN on small structured tabular datasets.

21. "Why not Decision Tree?"
    A single decision tree is prone to overfitting — it memorises training
    data. Random Forest averages many trees to get a more generalised model.

22. "Why not SVM?"
    SVM is computationally expensive for multi-class (requires one-vs-one or
    one-vs-rest decomposition). SVM doesn't provide probability estimates
    natively (requires calibration). Random Forest gives better performance
    on structured tabular data.

23. "Why not KNN?"
    KNN is slow at prediction time (must compute distance to all training
    points). For a real-time web app, prediction speed matters. KNN is also
    sensitive to feature scaling and irrelevant features.

24. "Why not Logistic Regression?"
    Logistic Regression assumes linear separability. Disease classification
    often involves non-linear boundaries between classes. Random Forest
    handles non-linearity naturally.

25. "Why Deep Learning?"
    We did not use deep learning for disease prediction — we used Random
    Forest. The NCF deep learning model is designed for recommendation
    (not disease prediction) and is not currently active.

26. "Is your system really ML or just a rule-based DSS?"
    It is a hybrid. The disease prediction layer is ML (Random Forest).
    The risk classification, severity mapping, food filtering, and
    recommendation scoring are all rule-based DSS logic. Both components
    are present and serve different roles.

27. "What part of your project is DSS?"
    backend/risk.py (clinical reference range checks), severity_rules.py
    (severity constraint tables), config.py (disease thresholds), and the
    filtering + scoring logic in enhanced_recommender.py.

28. "What part is ML?"
    predict.py — three RandomForestClassifier predictions using pre-trained
    model .pkl files.

29. "What part is recommendation?"
    backend/services/enhanced_recommender.py — EnhancedNutritionRecommender
    class, generate_meal_plan() function. Also recommendation.py for
    foods_to_avoid, water_intake, protein_requirement, nutrition_tips.

30. "What is the difference between prediction and recommendation?"
    Prediction: takes patient health parameters and uses an ML model to output
    a disease category (Diabetes/Kidney Disease/etc.) — a classification task.
    Recommendation: takes the predicted disease + user profile, filters a food
    database, scores and ranks candidate foods, and outputs a personalised
    daily meal plan — a content matching/ranking task.

"So, our project is basically a health dashboard where a patient enters their
medical information — things like their HbA1c value, blood sugar, blood
pressure, creatinine, BMI — and the system does two things.

First, it predicts whether they have diabetes, obesity, or kidney disease.
We trained three separate Random Forest classifiers for this — one for each
disease. Random Forest works by building many decision trees and combining
their votes. We use it because it handles multi-class prediction well and
gives us a confidence percentage too.

The output of the prediction isn't just 'yes or no disease' — it goes through
a rule-based risk classifier that checks the patient's actual clinical values
against normal reference ranges. So if the model says diabetes but all the
blood markers are normal, we call it a 'Model Flag' rather than 'High Risk',
which protects the patient from unnecessarily strict diet restrictions.

This risk level then automatically becomes a severity — High Risk becomes
Severe, Moderate Risk becomes Moderate, and so on.

The second thing the system does is recommend a meal plan. We have a dataset
of 1,014 Indian foods with their nutritional values. Based on the detected
disease and severity, we filter out unsafe foods — for severe diabetes that
means removing anything with more than 2 grams of sugar. Then we score every
remaining food with a hybrid formula that accounts for the user's daily calorie
target, BMI, age, gender, and activity level.

The top-scoring foods are arranged into Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, and Dinner.

Now, the base paper we referenced used a pure DSS — you had to manually enter
the disease. We automated that step with ML. We also added automatic severity
detection and made the food scoring personalised based on the individual's
profile, which the original DSS didn't do."
